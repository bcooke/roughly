# Quantifiable Human Facts

Roughly investigates quantities about human lives: experiences, actions, circumstances, and preferences. The subject includes more than opinions. A person's having visited a place or entered a body of salt water can be a historical occurrence even if nobody records it.

A useful form is: among a specified population, how many people satisfy a defined condition at a specified time? A lifetime-experience condition also needs a cutoff. Membership in the population and the condition can change, so the quantity need not be constant to have a determinate value under its definition.

Definitions matter. “People alive at a given time” differs from “everyone ever born.” A geographical region needs a boundary; being “in” water needs an inclusion rule. Those choices define the proposition. They do not by themselves show whether evidence can recover its value.

Subjective phenomena also admit empirical questions. “Red is a better color” is an evaluative judgment. How many people prefer red under a specified comparison is a different claim, about those people's preferences. Defining the comparison does not make a poll response identical to a stable underlying preference.

A helpful philosophical parallel for that last distinction is Searle's separation of the subjectivity of a phenomenon from the possibility of objective claims about it. His [1995 discussion](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/1995/11/16/the-mystery-of-consciousness-part-ii/) concerns consciousness; applying the distinction here is an interpretation, not a claim that his theory defines Roughly's whole scope.
