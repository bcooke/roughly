# Participation design investigation

Roughly aims to make data freely accessible while reducing distortion introduced by the collection experience itself. These questions are open research and design work. On November 2, 2026, mandatory-account assumptions were reopened. No identity or results-exposure policy was selected.

## Results before responses

Seeing the current distribution may influence a participant's answer. Asking someone to predict other people's answers may also change the context in which they report their own preference. The effects may depend on the question, participant, wording, and presentation; their magnitude and direction should not be assumed.

Requiring an answer before revealing results has its own costs: someone who only wants to browse may submit an arbitrary answer to unlock information, decline to participate, or be excluded from the sample. Hiding results in the voting screen cannot establish that someone has never encountered them through a shared link, search result, or earlier visit.

| Candidate | Benefit to investigate | Cost or uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Browse results freely, offer a distinct answering path | Preserves public access and an intentional participation flow | Participants may have seen results already |
| Offer a neutral “answer first” path and an explicit “view results” choice | Allows an unprimed first encounter without forcing a response | Adds friction and may produce different participant groups |
| Require a response to reveal results | Delays exposure inside that flow | Can encourage low-effort answers and block free browsing |
| Separate prediction or guessing exercises | Could support reflection without mixing answer types | Must not contaminate the collection experience or be counted as a preference |

No option is adopted for real collection. As of November 2, 2026, the preview displays fictional aggregates and collects nothing. A prediction interaction is deferred. Stable result links in the preview demonstrate navigation, not an approved future results-exposure policy.

## Identity and honesty

“Anonymous” needs a specific meaning. Public display names, private accounts, operator access to identity, duplicate-participant prevention, and verified legal identity are separate design dimensions. Hiding a person's name publicly does not make their answers anonymous to the operator.

| Candidate | Benefit to investigate | Cost or uncertainty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No account requirement | Lower participation friction and potentially greater perceived privacy | Repeat participation and automation are harder to distinguish |
| Pseudonymous account | Continuity and one-response-per-account controls | An account is not proof of one person, honesty, or anonymity |
| Stronger proof of personhood or legal identity | May strengthen particular duplicate-participant controls | Exclusion, privacy exposure, trust, operational burden, and altered willingness to answer |

Verification does not itself establish that a response is truthful. Privacy assurances must describe actual storage, linkage, access, and retention. Sensitive answers in immutable event history deserve explicit design before collection. Do not collect identity documents merely because they might improve data.

## Evaluate before choosing

For each proposal, identify the error it aims to reduce, possible errors it introduces, and what evidence would change the decision. Useful measures can include completion, item nonresponse, answer changes, duplicate signals, and response distributions across experiences. Engagement alone is not a data-quality measure. A change in the distribution is not proof that one design is more truthful.

Any experiment needs a defined question, participant protections, a sampling plan, and an analysis plan. Exposure metadata may help interpret a study but cannot reverse bias or prove a participant's full exposure history. Expert review and community contributions are welcome; the goal is to develop defensible methods rather than turn provisional interface choices into methodology by accident.

## Research starting points

- [AAPOR best practices](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/best-practices/): question design, pretesting, sampling, mode effects, and transparency.
- [Pew Research Center on writing survey questions](https://www.pewresearch.org/writing-survey-questions/): wording and question-order effects.
- [AAPOR disclosure standards](https://aapor.org/standards-and-ethics/disclosure-standards/): information readers need to interpret a survey.

These sources inform the research agenda; they do not validate a particular Roughly participation design.
